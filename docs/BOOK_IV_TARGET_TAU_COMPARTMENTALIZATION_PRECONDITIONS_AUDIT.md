## 1. Executive Verdict

The architecture supports **compartmentalization preconditions** at bridge level through the structural capacity of branched heteropolymers to form **porous mesh compartments** — cross-linked network structures that partially enclose interior regions while allowing selective passage of small molecules.

The mechanism is architectural. The trivalent K=7 monomer (branch point) enables three-dimensional network growth. A sufficiently large branched heteropolymer with multiple K=7 nodes connected by K=6 linker segments forms a mesh-like structure with interior voids, pores, and a topologically connected boundary surface. The mesh is not a lipid bilayer — it is a covalently bonded polymer network with openings of a size set by the linker-segment length between branch points.

The porous mesh compartment produces:

1. **Inside/outside asymmetry:** The interior of the mesh is spatially distinct from the exterior. Objects inside the mesh are partially enclosed.

2. **Size-selective retention:** Objects larger than the mesh pore size (long polymer chains, duplexes, catalytic scaffolds) are retained inside. Objects smaller than the pore size (free monomers, short chains) can pass through. This creates a natural **size-selective semi-permeable boundary** — large information-bearing and catalytic objects are trapped while small feedstock monomers can enter.

3. **Parasite shielding:** A cross-catalytic network enclosed in a mesh compartment is protected from large external parasitic sequences (which cannot penetrate the mesh pores). Short parasitic fragments can enter, but they lack the catalytic scaffold structure to compete effectively. The protection is partial — based on size exclusion, not active defense — but structurally meaningful.

4. **Concentration advantage:** By retaining large functional molecules inside a small volume while allowing monomer influx from a large external pool, the mesh compartment creates a local concentration advantage for catalysts and templates. The effective internal concentration of functional objects is higher than it would be in open solution.

The compartmentalization-precondition threshold is **crossed at bridge level.** The architecture supports boundary-forming structures with inside/outside asymmetry, size-selective retention, partial parasite shielding, and concentration advantage. No new postulates are required. Thirteenth consecutive zero-cost upper-stack target.

The compartment is not a cell. It is not a lipid membrane. It is a porous polymer mesh with physical but not biological compartmentalization. The gap between a mesh compartment and a cell is substantial: active transport, energy-driven maintenance, regulated permeability, division, and growth are all absent. But the structural prerequisite — an enclosed space that preferentially retains functional objects while admitting feedstock — is present.

**Classification:** Bridge-level BSR. Compartmentalization preconditions crossed. Proto-cell organization audit justified.

## 2. Why Compartmentalization Is the Next Correct Gate

The catalytic-cycle audit identified two primary vulnerabilities: parasite invasion and feedstock dependence. Both are resolved or mitigated by compartmentalization.

In origins-of-life research, compartmentalization is one of the three pillars of minimal cellularity (along with replication/heredity and metabolism). The bridge architecture has heredity/selection/catalysis but not compartmentalization or metabolism. Compartmentalization is the next structural upgrade because:

1. It protects the established cross-catalytic network from parasites.
2. It concentrates functional molecules, enhancing reaction rates.
3. It creates a unit of selection (the compartment) above the individual sequence.
4. It is a prerequisite for proto-cellular organization.

### 4.1 The Branched Polymer Mesh (Primary Candidate)

The K=7 monomer (trivalent, 1s² 2s² 2p³) provides three bonding sites arranged in an approximately trigonal geometry. When multiple K=7 nodes are connected by K=6 linker segments (each K=6 is divalent, providing a linear bridge between two branch points), the resulting structure is a three-dimensional polymer network.

The key property: a network with enough cross-links becomes topologically closed — it forms a surface that separates an interior from an exterior. The minimum closed surface requires ~4–6 K=7 nodes (the tetrahedron and cube are the simplest closed polyhedra from trivalent nodes). The pore size is set by the linker length (number of K=6 monomers between adjacent K=7 nodes).

For a tetrahedral cage (4 K=7 nodes, 6 K=6 linker edges):
- Each linker is a chain of m K=6 monomers.
- The edge length is ~m × d_bond (where d_bond ~ 2–3 a₀).
- The pore size (opening in each triangular face) is ~m × d_bond.
- A single polymer chain or duplex of length > pore size cannot pass through.

### 4.2 Multi-Chain Cross-Linked Shell

Multiple branched chains can bond to each other through their unsaturated K=7 branch-point sites. If several branched chains are brought together and their free K=7 sites cross-link, the result is a larger, more robust mesh shell. The shell can be:
- Polyhedral (regular or irregular, depending on the network topology).
- Multi-layered (if enough material accumulates).
- Self-healing (if free K=7 sites on the boundary can capture passing monomers or chain segments).

### 4.3 Boundary-Forming Verdict

Branched polymer meshes and multi-chain cross-linked shells are structurally available boundary-forming candidates. The K=7 trivalent monomer is the critical enabler: its three bonding sites allow 3D network growth that can close topologically. Linear (K=6-only) chains cannot form boundaries.

## 5. Self-Association and Enclosure Audit

### 5.1 Can the Mesh Close?

For a branched network to enclose a region, it must form a topologically closed surface (a surface with no boundary — every edge is shared by two faces). The minimum requirements:

- At least 4 trivalent (K=7) nodes connected to form a tetrahedral cage: 4 nodes, 6 edges, 4 triangular faces. This is the simplest closed trivalent polyhedron.
- Each edge is a linker segment of K=6 monomers.
- Total monomer cost: 4 K=7 + 6m K=6 (for linker length m).

For m = 3 (three K=6 monomers per edge): total = 4 + 18 = 22 monomers for the simplest cage. This is within the bridge architecture's chain-length regime (N ~ 20 for identity-faithful replication in Regime I).

Complete closure is topologically achievable for networks with sufficient K=7 nodes and K=6 linkers.

## 6. Inside/Outside Asymmetry Audit

### 6.1 Physical Distinction

A closed mesh compartment creates a physical boundary. The interior is a connected region surrounded by the mesh. The exterior is everything outside. Objects inside the mesh experience:
- A confined volume (smaller than the bulk environment).
- A boundary that restricts passage of large objects.
- Higher local concentration (if functional objects are trapped inside).

### 6.2 Selective Retention

The mesh pores have a characteristic size set by the linker length m. Objects larger than the pore size cannot pass through the boundary:
- **Retained inside:** Long polymer chains (N > ~m), duplexes, catalytic scaffolds, multi-chain complexes — all the functional objects of the pre-biological scaffold.
- **Passes through:** Free monomers (single composite units), short chain fragments (N < m), individual reaction products.

This creates a natural **size-selective semi-permeability:** the compartment retains the information-bearing and catalytic machinery while allowing feedstock monomers to enter from the environment.

### 6.3 Concentration Effect

If a compartment of volume V_in retains N_func functional objects, and the external volume is V_ext ≫ V_in, the internal concentration is:

c_in = N_func / V_in ≫ N_func / V_ext = c_ext (if the same objects were dispersed)

The concentration enhancement is ~V_ext / V_in — potentially many orders of magnitude for a small compartment in a large environment.

### 6.4 Inside/Outside Verdict

Inside/outside asymmetry is structurally present. The mesh boundary creates a physical distinction between interior and exterior. Size-selective retention traps large functional objects inside while allowing small monomers to pass. The concentration advantage is substantial for small compartments in large environments.

## 7. Parasite-Shielding Audit

### 7.1 How Shielding Works

A parasitic sequence is a chain that benefits from the catalytic network (is replicated by a catalyst inside the compartment) but does not contribute catalytic function back. Parasites are typically long sequences (comparable in length to the functional templates and catalysts).

If the parasite is longer than the mesh pore size, it cannot enter the compartment from outside. Internal parasites (arising by mutation within the compartment) can still exploit the network, but external parasites are physically excluded.

### 7.2 Shielding Effectiveness

- **Large parasites (N > m):** Excluded by size. Cannot penetrate the mesh. The compartmentalized network is fully shielded from these.
- **Small parasitic fragments (N < m):** Can enter through pores. But short sequences lack the catalytic scaffold structure needed to compete with established long-chain catalysts. They may consume some monomers but cannot replicate efficiently without template machinery.
- **Internal parasites (mutations of existing functional sequences):** Can arise within the compartment. These are not shielded by the boundary — they are internal to the system. Their control requires group selection (functional compartments out-reproduce parasitized compartments) or higher-specificity catalytic discrimination.

Internal parasites require group-selection dynamics (functional compartments out-reproduce parasitized ones), which requires compartment-level replication — a higher-order process not yet established but structurally conceivable (compartment growth + fragmentation).

## 8. Feedstock and Flux Audit

### 8.1 The Permeability Tradeoff

A closed compartment must balance two competing requirements:
- **Retention:** Keep functional objects (templates, catalysts, duplexes) inside.
- **Exchange:** Allow feedstock (monomers) to enter and waste to exit.

The size-selective mesh naturally resolves this tradeoff: large objects are retained, small objects pass through. The tradeoff is set by the pore size m:
- **Large pores (m large):** Good monomer flux, but poor retention of medium-sized objects.
- **Small pores (m small):** Excellent retention, but restricted monomer flux (diffusion-limited).
- **Optimal pores:** Retain all functional chains (N > m) while admitting all monomers (N = 1 < m).

Since functional chains are typically N ~ 10–20 and monomers are N = 1, any pore size 2 ≤ m ≤ 8 provides the correct separation. This range is structurally available.

### 8.2 Flux Characteristics

- **Monomer influx rate:** Set by diffusion through pores. Proportional to the number of pores, the pore area, and the external monomer concentration. The mesh geometry provides many pores (one per face of the polyhedron).
- **Internal reaction rate:** Set by the internal catalyst concentration and the catalytic rate constants. Enhanced by the concentration advantage.
- **Steady-state flux:** If monomer influx balances consumption by internal replication/catalysis, the compartment reaches a steady state where it continuously operates. If consumption exceeds influx, the compartment eventually runs out of internal monomers. If influx exceeds consumption, monomers accumulate.

### 8.3 Flux Verdict

The porous mesh compartment supports a physically reasonable feedstock/flux regime. Monomers enter through pores, are consumed by internal replication and catalysis, and the compartment operates at steady state as long as the external monomer concentration is sufficient. The flux is diffusion-limited through the pores — a real physical constraint, but not a structural blocker.
