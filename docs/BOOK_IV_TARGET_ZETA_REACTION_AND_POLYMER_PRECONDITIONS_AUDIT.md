## 2. Why Reaction/Polymer Preconditions Are the Next Correct Gate

The chemistry-entry capstone established that bonded composite pairs exist with classifiable bond types and composite diversity. But a single bond between two composites is a dimer, not a chemistry. Chemistry requires transformations (reactions), extended structures (polymers/materials), and — for biological relevance — information-carrying capacity (sequences).

The gap between "bonded pair" and "information-bearing material" spans three rungs:
1. Can bonds form and break dynamically? (Reaction precondition)
2. Can bonding repeat into extended structures? (Polymer precondition)
3. Can extended structures carry ordering information? (Sequence precondition)

This audit tests all three rungs in one pass, because they share the same structural inputs (bond grammar + composite diversity) and their answers determine whether the upper-stack biological information program can open.

## 4. Reaction-Channel Audit

### 4.1 Bond Formation

Two unbonded open-shell composites approach each other. If an attractive bonding channel exists (covalent-like delocalization, ionic-like transfer), the system can transition from the unbound to the bound state. The transition releases binding energy (exothermic bond formation). The reverse process (bond breaking) requires supplying at least the bond dissociation energy.

**Status:** Bond formation is structurally available for any pair of composites with compatible valence structure. The K=3 + K=3 covalent-like bond was demonstrated in the Target Delta audit. Bond formation for other pairs (K=5+K=5, K=3+K=9, etc.) follows the same mechanism with different orbital and energy specifics.

### 4.2 Bond Breaking

A bonded pair can dissociate if sufficient energy is supplied to overcome the binding energy D_bond. The dissociation products are two separate composites, each recovering its independent shell configuration. Bond breaking is the time-reverse of bond formation and is kinematically available whenever the system's total energy exceeds the dissociation threshold.

**Status:** Structurally available. No barrier analysis has been performed (activation energies are uncomputed), but the existence of a bound-state potential well with finite depth guarantees that dissociation is energetically possible.

### 4.3 Constituent Transfer (Exchange Reactions)

A constituent can transfer from one composite to another if the transfer lowers the total energy. Example: in a K=3 + K=9 encounter, the K=3 composite's valence constituent (2s¹) can transfer to the K=9 composite, producing K=2 (closed shell) + K=10 (closed shell). This is energetically favorable if the ionization energy of K=3 is less than the electron affinity of K=9 (the energy gained by K=9 closing its shell).

**Status:** Structurally available for asymmetric composite pairs where transfer achieves shell closure. This is the bridge-level analogue of ionic/exchange reactions.

### 4.4 Multiple Pathways

Consider two K=3 composites and one K=9 composite as starting material. Multiple reaction pathways exist:

- Pathway A: K=3 + K=3 → (K=3-K=3) covalent dimer; K=9 unchanged
- Pathway B: K=3 + K=9 → K=2 + K=10 (ionic transfer); second K=3 unchanged
- Pathway C: K=3 + K=9 → (K=3-K=9) polar bond; second K=3 unchanged

Different pathways produce different products with different energies. The system has reaction selectivity determined by the relative energetics of the pathways.

**Status:** Multiple pathways exist structurally. Selectivity is parameter-dependent but the grammar supports genuine reaction diversity.

## 5. Polymerization Candidate Audit

### 5.1 Multi-Bond Composites

For polymerization, a composite must be able to bond with more than one partner simultaneously. This requires the composite to have **two or more available bonding sites** — that is, two or more valence constituents that can independently participate in bonds with different neighboring composites.

**Key candidates:**

### Table 3 — Candidate Polymerizing Composite Types

| Composite | Configuration | Valence constituents | Available bonds | Bondability | Status |
|-----------|--------------|---------------------|----------------|------------|--------|
| K=3 (1s² 2s¹) | 1 valence (s) | 1 s-type | 1 (saturated after one bond) | **MONOVALENT** — can terminate a chain but not extend it | Present but limiting |
| K=5 (1s² 2s² 2p¹) | 1 valence (p) | 1 p-type (directional) | 1 (but directional) | **MONOVALENT directional** — can form σ-bond along one axis | Present |
| **K=6 (1s² 2s² 2p²)** | 2 valence (p) | 2 p-type | **2** (two independent p-bonds possible) | **DIVALENT** — can bridge between two neighbors | **KEY POLYMERIZATION CANDIDATE** |
| **K=7 (1s² 2s² 2p³)** | 3 valence (p) | 3 p-type | **3** (three orthogonal p-bonds) | **TRIVALENT** — can form branching or planar structures | **KEY BRANCHING CANDIDATE** |
| K=8 (1s² 2s² 2p⁴) | 4 valence (p) but 2 unpaired | 2 p-type | 2 | Divalent (two bond sites, two lone pairs) | Present |
| K=4 (1s² 2s²) | 0 valence (subclosed) | 0 | 0 (inert at leading order) | Essentially closed | Not useful for polymerization |

### 5.2 The K=6 Divalent Candidate

The K=6 composite (bridge-level "carbon-like" analogue, configuration 1s² 2s² 2p²) is the simplest composite with two independent bonding sites. Its two unpaired 2p constituents can form two separate bonds — one on each side — making it a natural chain-forming monomer.

A chain of K=6 composites bonded through their p-orbitals would produce a **linear polymer-like structure**: ...−K6−K6−K6−K6−...

Each K=6 acts as a divalent linker. The p-orbital directionality provides geometric preference for the chain axis. The chain is held together by the same covalent-like bonding mechanism demonstrated in Target Delta, applied repeatedly.

### 5.3 The K=7 Trivalent Candidate

The K=7 composite (1s² 2s² 2p³, three unpaired p-constituents in orthogonal orbitals) has three bonding sites. This permits **branching**: a K=7 node can connect to three neighbors in approximately orthogonal directions, producing network or sheet structures rather than simple linear chains.

### 5.4 Polymerization Verdict

Repeated bonding is structurally available. Divalent (K=6) and trivalent (K=7) composites can serve as chain-forming and branching monomers. Linear, branched, and network growth modes are all structurally accessible. The polymerization precondition is crossed.

## 7. Stability vs Reactivity Audit

### 7.1 Stability Spectrum

The composite ladder spans a range from highly stable (closed-shell, inert) to highly reactive (open-shell, multiple bonding sites):

| Composite class | Reactivity | Suitability for chain formation |
|----------------|-----------|-------------------------------|
| Closed-shell (K=2, 10, 28) | Very low; inert | NOT suitable — will not bond |
| Monovalent open-shell (K=3, 5) | Low-moderate; one bond only | Chain terminators / end caps |
| **Divalent open-shell (K=6, 8)** | **Moderate; two bonds** | **Ideal chain-forming monomers** |
| **Trivalent open-shell (K=7)** | **Moderate-high; three bonds** | **Branching nodes** |
| High-valence (K=4 subshell edge) | Anomalous | Context-dependent |

### 7.2 The Goldilocks Zone

Polymerization requires monomers in the intermediate reactivity regime: reactive enough to form bonds repeatedly, but stable enough that the bonds persist once formed. The divalent composites (K=6, K=8) are in this zone: each forms two bonds, saturating its valence, and the resulting chain is stable because all bonding sites are occupied.

Trivalent composites (K=7) are slightly more reactive (three sites to fill) but can also reach saturation in network or branched structures. Monovalent composites (K=3, K=5) are too inert to sustain chain growth but serve as chain terminators.

### 7.3 Chain Stability

A chain of divalent composites is stable when:
- Every monomer's two bonding sites are occupied (saturated chain)
- The bond energies exceed the thermal/dissipative energy scale (bonds persist)
- The chain is not subject to spontaneous fragmentation (no internal weakness)

The saturation condition is the key stabilizer: a fully bonded chain has no unsatisfied valence, making it chemically inert along its length. Only the chain ends (if uncapped) or defect sites (if a bond is broken) are reactive.

### 7.4 Stability Verdict

The architecture supports the correct intermediate-reactivity regime for chain formation. Divalent and trivalent composites form stable saturated chains. The stability is conditional on bond energies exceeding the dissipative/thermal background (the same scale-separation condition inherited from the matter bridge).

## 8. Sequence-Bearing Structure Audit

### 8.1 The Central Question

Can a chain of bonded composites carry **ordering information** — meaning that the sequence of monomer types along the chain is a physical property that affects the chain's structure and behavior?

### 8.2 Why Sequence Matters Structurally

Consider a chain of length N containing two monomer types: A (K=6 divalent linker) and B (K=7 trivalent branch point). A chain with sequence AABAA is linear with a single branch at position 3. A chain with sequence ABAAB has branches at positions 2 and 5 with a different spacing. These two chains have different spatial geometries, different mechanical properties (flexibility, rigidity), and different available bonding sites (the B nodes have unsaturated bonds that can connect to other structures).

The sequence is not merely a label — it determines the chain's physical structure. This is the minimal requirement for a material object to be information-bearing: the arrangement of its parts matters for its behavior.

### Table 5 — Sequence-Bearing Structure Conditions

| Condition | Met? | Why it matters |
|-----------|------|---------------|
| Multiple distinct monomer types | **YES** | K=6, K=7, K=8 (and others) have different bonding properties |
| Sequence affects chain geometry | **YES** | Branch-point placement changes spatial structure |
| Sequence affects chain bonding capability | **YES** | More branch points → more unsaturated sites → more reactivity |
| Sequence is persistent | **CONDITIONAL** | Sequence is fixed once chain is assembled; persists as long as bonds persist |
| Sequence is readable | **STRUCTURAL** | Different sequences produce different spatial/bonding signatures; in principle distinguishable |
| Sequence is writable | **STRUCTURAL** | Chain assembly from a monomer pool can produce arbitrary sequences |
| Sequence enables combinatorial diversity | **YES** | For N monomers of k types: k^N possible sequences; exponential diversity |

### 8.3 Combinatorial Capacity

A chain of N monomers drawn from k distinct types has k^N possible sequences. For even modest values (k = 3 types, N = 20 monomers), this produces 3^20 ≈ 3.5 × 10⁹ distinct sequences — a combinatorial explosion of structural diversity from a small alphabet and moderate chain length.

This combinatorial capacity is the structural foundation of biological information storage. DNA uses k = 4 (nucleotide bases) and N ~ 10⁶ to 10⁹ to encode the information content of living organisms. The bridge architecture does not replicate these specific numbers, but it provides the same combinatorial principle: finite alphabet × chain length = exponential information capacity.

### 8.4 What Sequence-Bearing Does NOT Mean

Sequence-bearing structure is not coding. Coding requires:
- A mapping from sequence to function (e.g., codon → amino acid)
- A reading mechanism (e.g., ribosome)
- A copying mechanism (e.g., DNA polymerase)
- Error correction (e.g., proofreading enzymes)

None of these is present in the bridge architecture. The architecture provides the material substrate on which coding could in principle operate, but not the coding machinery itself.

### 8.5 Sequence-Bearing Verdict

Sequence-bearing structure is **available** at bridge level. Chains of mixed-monomer composites carry ordering information that affects their physical properties. The combinatorial capacity is exponential in chain length. This is the structural prerequisite for information-bearing material, not information processing itself.
