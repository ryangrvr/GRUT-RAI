## 1. Executive Verdict

The architecture supports **growth and division preconditions** at bridge level. The proto-cell compartment can grow through internal production of boundary material, develop shape instabilities at larger size, fragment through mechanically plausible division modes, and produce daughters with statistically viable internal content. A complete reproductive cycle — feedstock import → internal production → boundary growth → division → daughter continuation — is structurally closed.

**Growth** is structurally direct: the assembly catalysts (established in Target Phi) produce functional monomers (K=6, K=7) from imported solitons. K=6 and K=7 are the same composite types that form the mesh boundary (K=6 divalent linkers + K=7 trivalent branch points). Internally produced K=6 and K=7 units can be incorporated into the mesh boundary at sites where free bonding sites are exposed (boundary defects, edges of incomplete polyhedra, or actively growing boundary regions). The boundary grows by the same bonding grammar that built it in the first place.

**Division** follows from a generic physical principle: a closed mesh shell that grows beyond a critical size develops mechanical strain. A polyhedral cage with N nodes has internal volume scaling as N^(3/2) while surface area scales as N. As the internal content (chains, duplexes, monomers) accumulates through production and replication, the internal pressure (effective osmotic pressure from the concentration of large retained objects) grows. When internal pressure exceeds the boundary's mechanical yield strength, the mesh deforms — developing necks, elongations, or weak points — and eventually fragments.

The most plausible division mode is **binary fission through neck formation:** an overgrown compartment elongates under internal pressure, develops a neck (a constriction where the mesh is thinnest), and pinches off into two daughter compartments. Each daughter inherits approximately half the internal content (templates, catalysts, assembly scaffolds, monomers) and approximately half the boundary material. The partitioning is **statistical, not regulated** — daughters receive a random sample of the parent's contents, biased by spatial distribution at the time of fission.

**Daughter viability** depends on whether each daughter inherits a sufficient subset of functional components. If the parent contains many copies of each essential functional sequence (templates, replication catalysts, assembly catalysts), statistical partitioning ensures that most daughters receive at least one copy of each type. The viability condition is:

**n_copies × (probability of inheriting at least one) > threshold**

For a parent with ~10 copies of each of ~4 essential sequence types, random binary partitioning yields viable daughters with probability > 90%. Daughters with incomplete functional sets are nonviable and are eliminated by selection — proto-cells with more copies of essential sequences before division produce more viable daughters.

The reproductive cycle is **structurally closed but unregulated.** Growth, division, and daughter continuation all occur, but the timing, geometry, and content distribution of division are not controlled by internal feedback. Division happens when the boundary mechanically fails, not when the internal content is optimally distributed. This produces variable daughter quality — some viable, some not — with natural selection favoring proto-cells whose growth/division dynamics produce more viable offspring.

No new postulates are required. Sixteenth consecutive zero-cost upper-stack target.

**Classification:** Bridge-level BSR. Growth/division preconditions crossed. Reproductive cycle structurally closed. Unregulated division with statistical content inheritance. Proto-life reproduction preconditions established.

The extended capstone identified growth and division as the next decisive boundary because reproduction transforms a persistent system into a lineage of systems. Without reproduction, a single proto-cell lives and dies. With reproduction, proto-cells form lineages — and selection can act at the compartment level (functional proto-cells out-reproduce dysfunctional ones), not just at the individual-sequence level.

Compartment-level reproduction is also the mechanism that resolves the internal-parasite vulnerability identified in Target Sigma: if functional proto-cells reproduce and parasitized proto-cells do not, parasites are eliminated by group selection.

## 5. Growth-to-Shape-Instability Audit

### 5.1 The Volume-Pressure Mechanism

As the proto-cell's internal content grows (through replication, catalysis, and monomer assembly), the number of large retained objects increases. These objects exert an effective osmotic pressure on the boundary:

**P_internal ∝ N_retained / V_internal**

where N_retained is the number of retained large chains/duplexes and V_internal is the compartment volume.

If internal production (which increases N_retained) outpaces boundary growth (which increases V_internal), the internal pressure rises. Eventually P_internal exceeds the boundary's mechanical yield strength, and the boundary deforms.

### 5.2 Shape Instability Modes

A spherical or near-spherical mesh shell under uniform internal pressure is mechanically stable. But beyond a critical pressure or size, several instability modes become available:

**Elongation:** The shell stretches along one axis, becoming ellipsoidal. This is energetically favorable when the internal pressure exceeds the isotropic restoring force of the mesh.

**Neck formation:** An elongated shell develops a constriction (neck) at its middle. The neck region has the highest curvature and highest local stress. This is the precursor to binary fission.

**Budding:** A localized region of the boundary bulges outward, forming a bud connected to the parent by a narrow stalk. The bud can eventually pinch off.

**Mesh fracture:** The boundary cracks at a structural weak point (a region with fewer cross-links or a boundary defect). The crack can propagate, splitting the shell.

### 5.3 Critical Size

The critical size for shape instability depends on:
- The mesh's mechanical properties (stiffness, yield strength) — set by bond energies and network connectivity.
- The internal pressure — set by the rate of content accumulation.
- The boundary growth rate — set by the rate of K=6/K=7 production and incorporation.

When internal content growth outpaces boundary growth: pressure builds → shell deforms → division becomes mechanically favorable.

### 5.4 Shape-Instability Verdict

Growth-to-shape-instability is **structurally plausible.** The volume-pressure mechanism provides a generic physical driver for shell deformation beyond a critical size. Elongation, neck formation, budding, and fracture are all standard mechanical instability modes for pressurized elastic shells. The specific critical size depends on parameters (mesh stiffness, production rates) that are not computed but are structurally well-defined.

## 6. Division-Mode Audit

### Table 3 — Division-Mode Outcomes

| Mode | Plausible? | Daughter quality | Status |
|------|-----------|-----------------|--------|
| **Binary fission (neck pinch-off)** | **YES** | Two roughly equal daughters; each inherits ~half of contents and boundary | **PRIMARY MODE** — generic for pressurized elastic shells |
| **Budding (asymmetric)** | **YES** | One large parent + one small bud; bud may be under-equipped | **SECONDARY** — produces unequal daughters |
| **Mesh fracture (catastrophic)** | **YES but unfavorable** | Fragments of irregular size; boundary integrity may be lost | **TERTIARY** — possible but daughters less likely viable |
| **Random shattering** | NO (excluded by definition) | Debris; no functional daughters | Below threshold |
| **Controlled/regulated division** | NO | Would require internal feedback machinery | Not available (no regulatory architecture) |

### 6.1 Binary Fission as Primary Mode

Binary fission through neck formation is the most plausible division mode because:
1. It is the generic mechanical instability of pressurized elastic shells (well-studied in biophysics and materials science).
2. It naturally produces two roughly equal halves.
3. It preserves boundary continuity — each daughter retains a closed (or nearly closed) mesh shell.
4. It distributes internal contents approximately evenly between daughters.

The fission process: elongated shell → neck forms at the midpoint → neck narrows under continued internal pressure → neck breaks → two daughter shells seal at the break points (free bonding sites at the break can re-bond with nearby mesh elements).

### 6.2 Resealing

After fission, the daughter shells have open edges at the break points. These edges expose free K=7 bonding sites that can capture available K=6/K=7 units (from internal production or from the break debris), re-closing the boundary. Resealing is not guaranteed — it depends on the availability of boundary material near the break — but it uses the same bonding grammar as boundary growth and is structurally consistent.

### 7.1 Statistical Inheritance

Content partition at binary fission is statistical: each daughter receives a random sample of the parent's internal contents, determined by where each object happens to be located at the moment of fission. For a parent with N copies of a particular essential sequence type, the probability that a daughter receives at least one copy is:

**P(at least one) = 1 − (1/2)^N**

| N copies in parent | P(daughter gets ≥1) |
|--------------------|-------------------|
| 1 | 0.50 |
| 2 | 0.75 |
| 4 | 0.94 |
| 8 | 0.996 |
| 16 | 0.99998 |

For a parent with 4 essential sequence types and ≥4 copies of each, the probability that a daughter inherits at least one of every type is approximately (0.94)⁴ ≈ 0.78. With ≥8 copies each: (0.996)⁴ ≈ 0.98.

### 7.2 The Copy-Number Threshold

The key viability condition is that the parent must accumulate enough copies of each essential functional sequence before division. Proto-cells with higher pre-division copy numbers produce more viable daughters. This is a selectable trait: variants whose replication dynamics produce higher copy numbers before the growth-pressure threshold is reached are selected for at the proto-cell level.

### 7.3 Partition Verdict

Proto-cells with higher copy numbers of essential sequences produce viable daughters with higher probability. Copy-number management is a selectable trait.

## 8. Daughter Viability Audit

### 8.1 Viability Conditions

A daughter proto-cell is viable if it inherits:
1. At least one template of each essential sequence type.
2. At least one replication catalyst.
3. At least one assembly catalyst (for internal monomer production).
4. A closed or nearly-closed boundary (for retention and protection).
5. Sufficient free monomers to bootstrap initial replication rounds.

### 8.2 Viability Assessment

Given statistical partitioning with ≥4 copies of each essential type:
- Probability of inheriting all essential templates: ~0.78 (from Section 7.1)
- Probability of inheriting replication catalyst: ~0.94 (if ≥4 copies)
- Probability of inheriting assembly catalyst: ~0.94 (if ≥4 copies)
- Boundary: approximately half inherited; resealing plausible.
- Monomers: approximately half inherited; supplemented by continued soliton influx.

Overall estimated viability: **~70–90%** for daughters of well-equipped parents (≥4–8 copies of each essential type).

### 8.3 Nonviable Daughters

Daughters that fail to inherit essential components are nonviable:
- Missing templates: cannot replicate.
- Missing replication catalyst: can template but not at catalyzed rate; severely disadvantaged.
- Missing assembly catalyst: cannot produce monomers; reverts to purely consumptive mode; may persist temporarily on inherited monomer inventory.
- Broken boundary: contents leak; loses compartmentalization advantage.

Nonviable daughters are eliminated by selection: they produce fewer (or no) descendants, and their resources are recycled by the environment.

### 8.4 Viability Verdict

Daughter viability is **statistically ensured for well-equipped parents.** The viability probability increases with pre-division copy number, which is itself a selectable trait. Division produces a mixture of viable and nonviable daughters; selection favors lineages that maximize viable-daughter production.
