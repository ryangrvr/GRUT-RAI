## 1. Executive Verdict

The architecture supports **proto-Darwinian selection preconditions** at bridge level. Sequence-dependent differential success arises from three independent structural sources — differential duplex stability, differential replication efficiency, and differential persistence under noise — all of which are free consequences of the existing four-class pairing and bonding grammar without new postulates.

The mechanism is straightforward. Different four-class sequences produce different chain geometries (branch patterns, backbone rigidity, secondary-bonding site distribution). These geometric differences translate into:

1. **Differential duplex stability:** Sequences with higher D1↔A1 content (the geometrically tighter pairing) form more stable duplexes than sequences with higher D2↔A2 content (the geometrically looser pairing). More stable duplexes are better information stores but harder to separate for re-templating. Less stable duplexes separate more readily but are more vulnerable to degradation.

2. **Differential replication efficiency:** Templates with well-spaced, geometrically accessible pairing sites guide complement assembly more efficiently than templates with sterically crowded or poorly accessible sites. The replication rate is sequence-dependent because the local geometry of the template-partner contact varies with monomer identity and context.

3. **Differential persistence:** Chains whose bonding pattern produces a more rigid, well-connected backbone persist longer in the dissipative background than chains with structural weak points (isolated single bonds, strained geometry, high branch density). Persistence is sequence-dependent because the bonding grammar maps sequence to structure.

These three sources of differential success are independent, reproducible, and heritable: a variant that templates faster (or persists longer, or forms more stable duplexes) passes those properties to its descendants because the properties follow from the inherited sequence.

Competition arises automatically from shared resource pools. All lineages draw from the same monomer supply. A lineage that templates faster or persists longer consumes more monomers and produces more copies per unit time. Over many rounds, faster/more persistent lineages grow exponentially at the expense of slower/less persistent ones. This is not neutral drift — it is sequence-dependent competitive bias.

The proto-Darwinian selection threshold is crossed: inherited variants experience differential success that produces nonrandom lineage expansion. The system supports the most primitive form of selection: differential reproduction based on heritable structural properties, without metabolic energy input, without functional coding, and without organismal-level adaptation.

No new postulates are required. Ninth consecutive zero-cost upper-stack target.

**Classification:** Bridge-level BSR. Proto-selection preconditions crossed. Adaptation/fitness-proxy audit justified.

## 2. Why Proto-Selection Is the Next Correct Gate

The heredity audit established that variant lineages can persist and branch. But heredity without differential success is neutral drift — random wandering through sequence space with no direction. Selection requires that some variants do better than others in a systematic, heritable way.

The question is not whether variants exist (they do) but whether variants differ in performance. If all sequences replicate equally well and persist equally long, the system is hereditary but not selective. If sequences differ in replication rate, stability, or persistence — and those differences are heritable — the system crosses from neutral heredity into proto-selection.

- **Error-driven extinction without selective asymmetry:** If lineages die because of accumulated errors (Eigen threshold), that is an error catastrophe, not selection (unless some sequences resist error accumulation better than others).

## 6. Competition Audit

### 6.1 Resource Competition

All replicating lineages share the same monomer pool. The pool has finite monomer supply. As lineages replicate, they consume monomers. A lineage that replicates faster consumes monomers at a higher rate, depleting the pool available to slower lineages.

### 6.2 Competitive Dynamics

In a finite monomer pool with two competing lineages (fast replicator F and slow replicator S):

- F produces copies at rate r_F per template per unit time
- S produces copies at rate r_S per template per unit time (r_S < r_F)
- Both draw from the same monomer pool of size M

When M is abundant, both grow exponentially at their respective rates. When M becomes limiting, the faster replicator crowds out the slower: F captures a larger share of the remaining monomers because it templates faster.

The long-time composition of the population is:

**fraction(F) / fraction(S) ~ (r_F / r_S)^n** after n rounds

This ratio grows exponentially in the number of rounds. Even a small replication-rate advantage (r_F = 1.01 × r_S) produces a large population-fraction advantage over many rounds.

### Table 3 — Competition Modes

| Mode | Present? | Mechanism | Implication |
|------|----------|-----------|------------|
| **Monomer-pool competition** | **YES** | All lineages draw from shared finite pool; faster replicators consume more | Differential growth rates → population composition shifts |
| **Template-site competition** | **YES** | Free monomers attach to whichever template is available; faster templates occupy monomers first | Rate advantage amplified by occupancy effects |
| **Persistence competition** | **YES** | More persistent chains survive longer; their templates remain available for more rounds | Stability advantage → more cumulative copies |
| **Duplex-stability competition** | **YES** | More stable duplexes retain information better; less stable duplexes degrade, losing templates | Information-retention advantage → long-term lineage survival |
| **Niche partitioning** | **OPEN** | Different sequences might preferentially use different monomer subtypes, reducing direct competition | Possible but uncharacterized |

### 6.3 Competition Verdict

Competition is structurally present and unavoidable whenever multiple lineages share a finite monomer pool. The competition is not imposed from outside — it is an automatic consequence of shared resources and differential replication rates. Faster and more persistent lineages expand at the expense of slower and more fragile ones. This is genuine competitive dynamics, not neutral coexistence.

## 8. Selection-Bias Audit

### 8.1 Does Differential Success Integrate into Lineage Bias?

Yes. The mechanism is exponential amplification of small differences:

A lineage with replication rate r replicates exponentially: copies ~ 2^(r × t). A competing lineage with rate r' < r grows as 2^(r' × t). The ratio after t rounds is:

**ratio = 2^((r − r') × t)**

Even a small rate difference Δr = r − r' produces a large ratio over many rounds. The bias is:
- **Systematic:** correlated with heritable sequence properties, not random.
- **Amplifying:** small per-round differences compound exponentially.
- **Persistent:** the advantage is inherited by descendants.

### 8.2 Bias vs Drift Threshold

In a finite population of N_pop sequences, random drift produces fluctuations of order √N_pop. Selection bias dominates drift when:

**selection advantage × N_pop ≫ 1**

For even modest replication-rate differences (r_F/r_S = 1.01) in populations of hundreds of chains, selection dominates drift. The architecture supports this regime whenever the monomer pool is large enough to sustain many concurrent lineages.

### Table 5 — Selection-Bias Conditions

| Condition | Met? | Why it matters |
|-----------|------|---------------|

| Small differences amplify over rounds | **YES** | Exponential growth amplifies Δr into exponential population ratios |
| Bias is systematic, not random | **YES** | Performance follows from sequence, which is inherited |
| Bias dominates drift in reasonable populations | **YES (CONDITIONAL)** | Requires population large enough that selection advantage × N_pop ≫ 1 |
| Bias persists across generations | **YES** | Descendants inherit the sequence and therefore the performance |

### 8.3 Selection-Bias Verdict

Selection-like bias is structurally present. Small heritable performance differences compound into large lineage-composition changes over many rounds. The bias is systematic (correlated with sequence), amplifying (exponential growth differences), and persistent (inherited). In populations large enough for selection to dominate drift, the system exhibits proto-Darwinian selection dynamics.
