## 1. Executive Verdict

The many-body sector of the bridge architecture supports a **structural shell-filling grammar** that produces an atomic-number-like progression at bridge level. The ingredients — fermionic exchange antisymmetry, discrete orbital levels, spin degeneracy, and gauge-neutrality constraints — combine to produce countable occupation rules, closed-shell configurations, and a systematic correspondence between constituent number and shell occupancy.

The shell-filling grammar is structurally real but differs from the Standard Model periodic table in fundamental ways. The bridge architecture has identical-mass constituents (no electron/nucleus mass hierarchy), an SU(2) gauge group (not U(1) electromagnetism), and adjoint-representation matter (not fundamental-representation electrons). These differences mean that the shell capacities, closed-shell numbers, and binding patterns are different from real atomic physics. What is preserved is the organizing principle: fermionic exclusion forces sequential occupation of higher-energy orbitals, producing a series of composites indexed by constituent number with systematically different internal structures.

The architecture supports gauge-neutral composites for even numbers of constituents (pairs combining into singlets). Odd-constituent composites carry residual gauge charge and can participate in further binding — the bridge analogue of ions. Closed-shell configurations at specific constituent numbers exhibit enhanced stability relative to their neighbors, providing the first bridge-level analogue of magic numbers.

Chemistry-entry remains unjustified. The shell-filling grammar provides the organizational framework but not the specific content: no electron/nucleus distinction, no Coulomb potential with Z-dependent depth, no realistic spectral series, and no bonding theory. The many-body audit strengthens the atomic-structure analogue case but does not cross the chemistry threshold.

**Classification:** Bridge-level BSR. Many-body shell-filling grammar established. Atomic-structure analogue audit justified.

## 3. Many-Body Problem Setup

### 3.1 The Constituents

The building blocks are bridge-level spin-1/2 fermionic solitons in the adjoint representation of SU(2). Each soliton carries:
- Position X_i ∈ ℝ³
- Gauge charge Q_i^a (adjoint, 3 components)
- Spin s_i = 1/2 (from FR/Hopf quantization)
- Hard-core radius R_sk

### 3.2 The Interaction

- **Gauge force:** SU(2) Yang–Mills exchange. Long-range 1/d. Attractive in singlet channel, repulsive in quintet.
- **Hard core:** Repulsive at d < R_sk between any pair.
- **Exchange antisymmetry:** Total wavefunction antisymmetric under exchange of any identical pair.

### 3.3 The Organizing Principle

The many-body problem is governed by two competing constraints:

1. **Gauge neutrality:** Stable composites must be gauge-neutral (singlet) to avoid long-range gauge interactions with the environment. This constrains which combinations of constituents are allowed.

2. **Fermionic exclusion:** Each constituent is a spin-1/2 fermion. No two identical fermions can occupy the same quantum state. In a central-force bound system, the available states are labeled by (n, ℓ, m_ℓ, m_s), and each state can hold at most one fermion.

### 3.4 The Central-Force Approximation

For a many-body composite of K constituents, the exact problem is a K-body quantum system with pairwise gauge interactions. In the mean-field (Hartree-Fock-like) approximation, each constituent moves in the average potential generated by all others. This produces an effective central-force problem for each constituent, with single-particle energy levels that can be filled sequentially according to the exclusion principle.

## 4. Occupation / Shell-Filling Audit

### 4.1 Single-Particle Orbitals

In the mean-field approximation, each constituent moves in an effective potential that is approximately Coulomb-like at large distance (from the collective gauge charge of all other constituents) and hard-core-excluded at short distance (from overlap with any neighbor). The single-particle orbitals are labeled by:

- **n:** principal quantum number (shell label)
- **ℓ:** orbital angular momentum (0, 1, 2, ... = s, p, d, ...)
- **m_ℓ:** magnetic quantum number (−ℓ to +ℓ)
- **m_s:** spin projection (+1/2 or −1/2)

### 4.2 Subshell Capacity

Each subshell (n, ℓ) can hold:

**Capacity(n, ℓ) = 2(2ℓ + 1)**

(2 spin states × (2ℓ+1) magnetic substates). This is the standard result for a central-force problem with spin-1/2 fermions.

| Subshell | ℓ | Capacity |
|----------|---|----------|
| s | 0 | 2 |
| p | 1 | 6 |
| d | 2 | 10 |
| f | 3 | 14 |

### 4.3 Shell Capacity

Each shell n contains subshells ℓ = 0, 1, ..., n−1. The total capacity of shell n is:

**Capacity(n) = Σ_{ℓ=0}^{n−1} 2(2ℓ+1) = 2n²**

| Shell n | Subshells | Total capacity |
|---------|-----------|---------------|
| 1 | 1s | 2 |
| 2 | 2s, 2p | 2 + 6 = 8 |
| 3 | 3s, 3p, 3d | 2 + 6 + 10 = 18 |
| 4 | 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f | 2 + 6 + 10 + 14 = 32 |

### Table 2 — Shell-Filling / Occupation Structure

| Shell | Subshells | Capacity per subshell | Total shell capacity | Cumulative total |
|-------|-----------|----------------------|---------------------|-----------------|
| n = 1 | 1s | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| n = 2 | 2s, 2p | 2, 6 | 8 | 10 |
| n = 3 | 3s, 3p, 3d | 2, 6, 10 | 18 | 28 |
| n = 4 | 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f | 2, 6, 10, 14 | 32 | 60 |

### 4.4 Filling Order

In the pure Coulomb problem (hydrogen-like), all subshells within a shell are degenerate, and the filling order is simply n = 1, 2, 3, .... With the hard-core modification (which breaks the ℓ-degeneracy), subshells within a shell split: higher ℓ is generally more tightly bound (opposite to multi-electron atoms, where screening produces the Aufbau order). The precise filling order depends on the details of the effective potential, which varies with composite size.

For the purpose of this audit, the qualitative result is: a definite filling order exists, determined by the effective potential, producing a sequential occupation of orbitals. The specific order (whether 3d fills before or after 4s) is parameter-dependent and not yet computed.

### 4.5 Shell-Filling Verdict

A complete shell-filling grammar exists at bridge level. Each subshell has a definite capacity (2(2ℓ+1)), each shell has a definite total capacity (2n²), and the fermionic exclusion principle forces sequential occupation. The cumulative closed-shell numbers are 2, 10, 28, 60, .... The filling order depends on the specific effective potential and is not identical to the Standard Model Aufbau rule, but the organizational framework is structurally the same.

## 5. Many-Body Neutrality Audit

### 5.1 Gauge-Neutrality for K Constituents

Each constituent is in the adjoint (I = 1) representation of SU(2). The tensor product of K adjoint representations decomposes into irreducible representations. A gauge-singlet (I = 0) component exists if and only if the trivial representation appears in the decomposition.

**For K = 2:** 3 ⊗ 3 = 1 ⊕ 3 ⊕ 5. Singlet: **YES.**
**For K = 3:** 3 ⊗ 3 ⊗ 3 = 1 ⊕ 3·3 ⊕ 2·5 ⊕ 7. Singlet: **YES.**
**For K = 4:** Contains singlet. **YES.**
**General K:** The tensor product of K copies of the adjoint representation of SU(2) always contains the singlet for K ≥ 2. For K = 1, no singlet (a single adjoint charge cannot self-neutralize).

### 5.2 Neutral vs Charged Composites

- **Even K:** Pairs of constituents can form pairwise singlets. The composite is most naturally gauge-neutral. These are the "atomic" composites.
- **Odd K:** One constituent remains unpaired. The composite carries residual gauge charge (adjoint or higher). These are the "ionic" composites — they interact at long range with other charged objects.

### Table 3 — Neutral Composite Ladder

| K (constituents) | Singlet possible? | Stability expectation | Analogy |
|------------------|-------------------|----------------------|---------|
| 1 | NO (adjoint charge) | Stable soliton (single particle) | Free ion |
| 2 | YES (I=0 singlet) | Bound positronium-like pair | Simplest "atom" |
| 3 | YES (singlet in 3³) | Possible but less favorable | Exotic composite |
| 4 | YES | Paired singlets or collective singlet | Helium-like |
| 5 | YES | Possible with residual complexity | Open-shell composite |
| 6 | YES | Three-pair structure or collective | Closed-subshell candidate |
| 2n (general even) | YES (n pairwise singlets) | Favorable for pairwise binding | Even-Z "atoms" |

### 5.3 Neutrality and Shell Filling Interaction

Gauge neutrality and shell filling cooperate: the most stable composites are those where (a) all gauge charge is neutralized (singlet) and (b) all occupied orbitals form complete subshells (closed shell). These two conditions reinforce each other for even K at closed-shell numbers.

### 5.4 Neutrality Verdict

Gauge-neutral composites exist for all K ≥ 2. The singlet channel is always available. Even-K composites are most naturally neutral (pairwise singlet pairing). The neutral composite ladder supports a genuine progression of composite sizes.

## 6. Binding Persistence Audit

### 6.1 Does Binding Persist as K Grows?

The central question is whether the effective binding per constituent remains favorable as more constituents are added.

**Favorable factors:**
- Gauge attraction: each new constituent interacts attractively (in the singlet channel) with all existing constituents. The total binding energy grows roughly as K(K−1)/2 (number of pairs).
- Exclusion: forces occupation of higher orbitals, preventing collapse. Each new constituent adds to a higher energy level but remains bound if the level energy is negative.
- Hard core: prevents spatial collapse even for large K. The composite size grows with K (more orbitals → larger extent).

**Unfavorable factors:**
- Orbital energy increases with n: higher shells have less binding energy per constituent (E_n ~ −1/n²).
- Non-Abelian gauge dynamics: at strong coupling, the SU(2) gauge theory may confine, fundamentally altering the binding picture. Confinement effects are not analyzed.
- Many-body correlations: the mean-field approximation may break down for large K. Exact many-body effects (configuration interaction, correlation energy) are uncomputed.

### 6.2 Qualitative Persistence Assessment

In the weak-coupling regime (α_g small, a₀ large), the Coulomb-like tower has many bound levels. Each level has energy E_n ~ −α_g² μ_red / (2n²). A constituent added to shell n is bound if E_n < 0, which is true for all finite n. Therefore:

**In the weak-coupling regime, binding persists for arbitrarily many constituents.** Each new constituent occupies the next available orbital and is bound (though with decreasing binding energy per constituent as n grows). The composite grows in size (each shell extends further) and decreases in binding energy per constituent (each shell is less tightly bound).

This is exactly the pattern of real atoms: the first few electrons are tightly bound (inner shells), the last electrons are loosely bound (outer shells), and the atom grows in size with Z. The bridge architecture reproduces this qualitative pattern.

### 6.3 Maximum Practical Composite Size

In practice, the composite is limited by:
- The outermost occupied shell must have negative binding energy: n < n_max ~ some function of α_g, μ_red, and hard-core corrections.
- Environmental effects (dissipative background, finite temperature, external fields) may ionize loosely bound outer-shell constituents.
- Confinement effects (if SU(2) confines) may fundamentally limit the composite size.

These limitations are parameter-dependent and unexplored. There is no in-principle limit to K in the mean-field Coulomb picture, but practical limits certainly exist.

### 6.4 Binding Persistence Verdict

Many-body binding persists in the weak-coupling regime. Each added constituent occupies the next available orbital with diminishing but nonzero binding energy. The composite grows in size and decreases in binding energy per constituent. Binding is qualitatively stable against K-growth. Quantitative limits depend on parameters and many-body corrections.

## 7. Closed-Shell / Open-Shell Analogue Audit

### 7.1 Closed-Shell Configurations

A closed-shell configuration occurs when all subshells up to some level are completely filled. The cumulative closed-shell numbers (from Section 4.3) are:

**K_closed = 2, 10, 28, 60, ...**

(corresponding to filling through n = 1, 2, 3, 4, ...).

At these constituent numbers, every occupied orbital is full. No unpaired spins. No partially filled subshells. The composite is maximally symmetric and (in the pure Coulomb picture) especially stable.

### 7.2 Closed-Shell Stability Enhancement

Closed-shell composites are expected to be especially stable because:
- All orbitals within each subshell are filled → no low-energy excitations (the gap to the next shell is the "ionization energy").
- No unpaired spins → the composite is a spin singlet (S_total = 0) and diamagnetic.
- The filled-shell configuration is a unique quantum state (no degeneracy → no Jahn-Teller instability).

The first closed-shell composite is K = 2 (1s² — the "helium" analogue). The second is K = 10 (1s² 2s² 2p⁶ — the "neon" analogue). These are the bridge-level magic numbers.

### 7.3 Open-Shell Properties

Open-shell composites (K not at a closed-shell number) have partially filled outermost subshells. These composites:
- May carry net spin (unpaired constituents).
- May carry net gauge charge (if K is odd or if the representation coupling does not produce a singlet).
- Have lower ionization energy than their closed-shell neighbors (the outermost constituent is loosely bound).
- May be chemically "reactive" in the bridge-level sense — they can bind with additional constituents to reach the next closed shell.

### 7.4 Closed/Open-Shell Verdict

The bridge architecture supports both closed-shell and open-shell composite analogues. Closed-shell numbers (2, 10, 28, 60, ...) are the bridge-level magic numbers, corresponding to complete filling of all subshells through shell n. The pattern is structurally analogous to atomic shell closure, though the specific numbers differ from real atomic physics (where magic numbers are 2, 10, 18, 36, 54, 86 due to the Aufbau filling order and spin-orbit coupling).

- **Stability oscillation:** Closed-shell composites are more stable than their open-shell neighbors.
- **Size growth:** Each new shell extends the composite's spatial extent.
- **Ionization-energy oscillation:** Closed-shell composites have high ionization energy; just-beyond-closed-shell composites have low ionization energy.

These properties are the structural backbone of the periodic table. The bridge architecture reproduces them in qualitative form.

## 9. Chemistry-Entry Re-Evaluation

### 9.1 What Chemistry-Entry Would Require

Chemistry is the science of how atoms combine into molecules through bonding. At minimum, chemistry-entry requires:

1. **Distinct atom types** with different properties (different Z, different shell configurations).
2. **Valence structure** — atoms with partially filled outer shells that can share or exchange constituents.
3. **Bonding mechanism** — a way for atoms to lower their energy by sharing outer-shell constituents.
4. **Molecular structure** — stable multi-atom configurations with specific geometry.
5. **Reaction dynamics** — pathways for rearranging atomic/molecular structure.

### 9.2 What the Bridge Architecture Provides

The many-body audit provides (1) distinct composite types indexed by K, with different shell configurations and different reactivity. It provides (2) valence-like structure — open-shell composites have partially filled outer shells. It suggests (3) a bonding analogue — if two open-shell composites can share outer-shell constituents to form a combined closed-shell system, this would lower the total energy.

What it does not provide: specific bonding calculations, molecular geometry predictions, or reaction dynamics. The bridge architecture has the structural prerequisites for bonding (distinct types + valence + energy-lowering mechanism) but has not demonstrated that bonding actually occurs or produces stable molecular structures.

### 9.3 Chemistry-Entry Verdict

Chemistry-entry is **closer but not yet justified** as a formal audit. The prerequisites for bonding are now structurally present (distinct types + valence + potential energy-lowering mechanism). But the bonding mechanism itself has not been demonstrated, and the specific content (which composites bond, in what geometry, with what energy) is entirely uncomputed.

The honest assessment: the architecture now has the **framework** for chemistry-like behavior but not the **content**. A chemistry-entry audit could be justified as an exploratory (Track C) investigation but not as a definitive program.
